# The Lighthouse at Harrowgate

## Low Tide

The tide went out of Harrowgate the way a guest leaves a party, slowly at first and then all at once. By six the harbour was a field of black mud and stranded boats, and the gulls had it to themselves.

Wren Aldous walked the sea wall with her collar up and her satchel full of maps that no longer agreed with the coast. Every spring the sea took a little more of the headland, and every spring the Admiralty sent her to measure what was left. She had stopped minding. Measuring was a kind of mourning, and she was good at it.

At the end of the wall the lighthouse stood with its back to the town. The locals called it the Ember, after the colour its lamp turned in fog, and they spoke of it the way they spoke of the weather: as something that had always been there and would not be argued with.

The door at its foot was open. It had not been open in eleven years.

## The Keeper's Ledger

Inside, the air smelled of paraffin and old rope. A ledger lay on the table by the stairs, its pages swollen with damp, its last entry dated the night the keeper disappeared.

Wren read it standing up. *Wind north-east, rising. Glass falling. Lamp lit at 4.12. A ship that is not on any chart is standing off the point and showing no lights.*

Below that, in a different hand, someone had written a single line in pencil: *She is still out there. Bring the maps.*

Wren closed the ledger, and then, because she was a cartographer and could not help herself, she opened it again and copied the line into her notebook, exactly as it was written.

# A Map With No Coast

## Ink and Salt

Her lodgings were above the chandler's, a narrow room with a slanting floor and a window that looked straight out to sea. She spread the maps across the bed and weighted their corners with whatever came to hand: a candlestick, a boot, the Admiralty's letter of instruction.

The oldest map was two hundred years old and showed a coast that was mostly guesswork. The newest was her own, drawn last spring with instruments she trusted. Between them the headland shrank like a sentence being edited, a word at a time, until it said almost nothing at all.

But there was something on the oldest map that was not on any of the others. A little way off the point, where the chart said only *deep water*, an unknown hand had drawn a small square and written beside it, in letters faded almost to nothing, the word *Ember*.

The ship in the ledger had been standing off the point when the keeper vanished, and it had shown no lights, and it was not on any chart. Wren sat on the edge of the bed for a long time, reading the word over and over again, while the tide came back in under the window and the room went dark around her because she had forgotten to light the candle.

## The Visitor

The knock came a little after nine. Wren opened the door to a woman in a sea captain's coat, rain running off her hat brim, who looked at the maps on the bed before she looked at Wren.

"Captain Ilse Varga," she said. "You've been in the lighthouse."

"The door was open."

"It was opened for you." The captain stepped inside without being asked and laid one gloved finger on the faded square. "My father drew that. He was the keeper. And I think he's the one who wrote to you."

# Where the Light Goes

## Storm Season

The storm arrived on the third night, as the glass had promised. Wren and the captain climbed the Ember's hundred and twelve steps with a lantern between them, and at the top they found the great lamp cold and the lens turned, very deliberately, to face the open sea.

Wren set the lantern down and walked a slow circle around the lens. Someone had wedged it with a folded strip of canvas so that it could not drift, and had scratched a bearing into the brass with the point of a knife.

Ilse touched the scratches the way you might touch a name on a headstone.

"He aimed it," Ilse said. "Not at the rocks. At something out there."

Wren unroled the oldest map on the gallery floor and she held it down flat with both of her hands against the wind, which was very strong and cold. If the square was realy a ship, then it had been anchored in the exact same place for two hundred years. If it was not a ship, she did not yet have a word for it.

Far out, past the point, a light answered.
