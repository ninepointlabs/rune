# The Book as a Machine for Remembering

Every technology of writing changes what a reader is expected to hold in their head. Before print, a book was rare enough that reading it often meant memorising it; after print, a reader could afford to forget, because the book would still be there tomorrow, word for word, in a thousand identical copies.

That last claim, identical copies, is the one historians have argued about most. The fixity of print was not a property of the press itself but something printers, booksellers and readers had to work hard to establish, and for a long time they did not trust it.

# Print and Its Readers

What changed first was not the text but the reader's relationship to it. Oral cultures organise knowledge so that it can be remembered: in formulas, in rhythm, in stories that carry instructions inside them. Once words sit still on a page, they can be scanned, indexed, compared and corrected, and thought begins to take the shape of the page.

Historians of the book have since traced how this new kind of reader was made, one edition, one bookshop and one library at a time.

# Conclusion

A word processor is a late chapter in the same story. It is a machine for remembering that also, at last, remembers how it was changed.
